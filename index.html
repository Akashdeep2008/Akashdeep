01THE PROJECT

THE CHALLENGE & THE OPPORTUNITY

Waste is not just a disposal problem.
It is a design problem.

Rapid urban growth, changing consumption and short product lifecycles are placing increasing pressure on municipal waste systems. A zero-waste approach shifts the focus from collecting and dumping waste to preventing it, keeping products in use and recovering materials safely.

02KNOW THE MATERIAL

Different waste streams.
Different solutions.

Correct classification helps protect workers and the public, improves material recovery and prevents hazardous items from entering ordinary waste systems. Select a category to explore its sources and recommended management pathway.

01 / EVERYDAY MATERIALS

Municipal solid waste

Everyday waste from homes, offices, shops and public spaces, including food scraps, paper, packaging, glass, metals and textiles.

Typical sources

Households, markets, offices, restaurants and street cleaning.

Recommended pathway

Separate wet organics, dry recyclables and domestic hazardous waste at the point of generation.

✓

Keep wet and dry waste separate; do not mix batteries, chemicals or sharps with household waste.

Important: Biomedical, chemical and other hazardous waste require regulated collection and specialist treatment. Follow local municipal guidance and applicable rules.

03WHY IT MATTERS

The impact of
mismanaged waste.

Waste left in open areas, burned without controls or disposed of in poorly managed sites can affect air, water, soil, ecosystems and community health. Preventing pollution requires action across the entire waste chain.

01 / AIR

Air pollution

Open burning and poorly controlled treatment can release particulate matter and harmful gases. Avoid burning waste and enforce emission controls where treatment facilities are used.

02 / WATER

Water contamination

Leachate and litter can pollute rivers, lakes and groundwater. Improve containment, drainage, collection and monitoring around disposal sites.

03 / SOIL

Soil degradation

Hazardous substances and mixed waste can damage soil quality and ecosystems. Keep hazardous streams separate and return clean organic matter through controlled composting.

04 / CLIMATE

Climate pressure

Organic waste decomposing without oxygen in landfills can produce methane. Divert suitable organics to composting or anaerobic digestion and prevent avoidable food waste.

THE KEY TAKEAWAY

Waste impacts are connected. Source segregation, reliable collection, safe treatment and reduced consumption work together; one intervention alone is not enough.

04THE SOLUTION FRAMEWORK

From linear use to
a circular city.

A zero-waste system prioritizes prevention and reuse before recycling. Residual waste should be minimized, while any treatment or disposal must protect people and the environment.

THE WASTE HIERARCHY

Keep materials
at their highest value.

Move down the hierarchy only when higher-value options are not practical or safe.

01

Refuse & reduce

Prevent unnecessary products and packaging; buy only what is needed and choose durable, repairable goods.

HIGHEST PRIORITY
02

Reuse, repair & refill

Extend product life through repair, sharing, refill systems, second-hand use and reusable serviceware.

03

Recycle materials

Collect clean, separated paper, metals, glass and accepted plastics for appropriate processing.

04

Compost & recover

Process suitable organic waste and recover materials or energy where technically and environmentally appropriate.

05

Treat & dispose safely

Manage unavoidable residues in controlled facilities; prevent open dumping and uncontrolled burning.

LAST RESORT
01 / ORGANIC RESOURCES

Separate organics. Return nutrients.

Food scraps and garden waste can be processed through well-managed composting or anaerobic digestion. Keep contaminants out, control moisture and aeration, and use the resulting product appropriately.

  • Household or community composting
  • Separate collection of food waste
  • Quality checks for finished compost
02 / DRY MATERIALS

Make segregation work end to end.

Separation succeeds only when bins, collection vehicles, sorting centres and end markets are aligned. Clear labels and consistent collection schedules help prevent contamination.

  • Separate dry recyclables at source
  • Provide safe, accessible collection points
  • Build transparent links to recyclers
03 / SYSTEM DESIGN

Design services around real data.

Map waste generation, optimize routes, maintain vehicles and publish service performance. Digital tools can support decisions but cannot replace reliable operations or public trust.

  • Waste audits and material-flow mapping
  • Collection planning and maintenance
  • Public reporting and accountability
05SMART WASTE MANAGEMENT

Technology should solve
a defined problem.

Artificial Intelligence and the Internet of Things can help cities understand waste flows, improve collection and recover materials. They should be introduced where the expected benefit justifies cost, maintenance and data needs.

01

IoT-enabled bins

Fill-level sensors can flag bins that need service and support route planning. Pilot first, check sensor reliability and ensure maintenance ownership.

USE CASE · COLLECTION EFFICIENCY
02

AI-assisted sorting

Computer vision and automated equipment can identify selected material types at sorting facilities. Performance depends on input quality, lighting, material variation and human supervision.

USE CASE · MATERIAL RECOVERY
03

Route optimization

Combine collection schedules, vehicle capacity, road conditions and service reports to reduce missed pickups and unnecessary travel.

USE CASE · LOGISTICS
04

Digital tracking & analytics

Track quantities collected, sorted, recycled, composted and landfilled. Use consistent definitions and verified records rather than unsupported claims.

USE CASE · TRANSPARENCY
05

Citizen reporting

Simple reporting channels can help residents flag missed collection, overflowing bins or illegal dumping, with clear response times and feedback.

06FROM PLAN TO PRACTICE

A city roadmap
that can be implemented.

Progress requires coordinated action by municipal bodies, residents, institutions, waste workers, producers and recyclers. Start with a clear baseline, pilot workable interventions and expand what performs well.

07INDIAN CITY CONTEXT

Learn from city
implementation models.

The project report identifies Indore and Surat as examples worth studying. Their relevance is the need to combine public participation, source segregation, dependable services, monitoring and institutional coordination. Outcomes should be checked against current official records before quoting figures.

CASE STUDY 01 · MADHYA PRADESH

Indore

Study the role of door-to-door collection, source segregation, public communication, cleanliness systems and monitoring. A transferable lesson is that infrastructure and daily service discipline must work together.

Questions to investigate

  • How is segregated waste collected and transported?
  • How are citizens informed and service quality monitored?
  • How are different waste streams processed?
CASE STUDY 02 · GUJARAT

Surat

Explore how municipal planning, collection services, public-health priorities, infrastructure and technology can be coordinated in a rapidly growing urban area.

Questions to investigate

  • How are service gaps identified and addressed?
  • What role do data and operational monitoring play?
  • How can recycling and resource recovery be strengthened?

These are study prompts, not a comparative ranking. Verify current city-specific details using municipal or government publications before adding numerical claims.

10CONCLUSION

THE WAY FORWARD

A zero-waste city is built
one connected system at a time.

The transition begins by preventing unnecessary waste and separating materials at source, then connecting each stream to reliable reuse, recycling, composting or safe treatment. Technology can strengthen these systems, but lasting progress depends on good planning, transparent measurement, capable services and participation from every part of the city.
